Project description · Model and numerics

How SPICE works

SPICE is a controlled experiment in galaxy formation and cosmic reionisation. Three radiation-hydrodynamics simulations begin from the same density field and use the same cosmology, resolution, thermochemistry, star-formation model, stellar populations, radiation transport, and feedback-coupling algorithm. Only the timing and energy distribution of supernova explosions change.

Following corresponding haloes across the three runs lets us trace that single change from star-forming gas, through galaxy structure and photon escape, to the ionisation state of the intergalactic medium.

01 · Scientific design

The controlled comparison.

Matched initial conditions, different supernova models

The experiment asks whether identical haloes can develop different star-formation histories, internal structures, and reionisation environments solely because supernova energy is delivered differently.

Supernovae return mass, metals, thermal energy, and radial momentum to the interstellar medium. The response is non-linear: explosions that occur close together can overlap before their remnants cool, building a hot volume-filling phase and coherent outflows. More widely separated events act on gas that has had time to cool, collapse, or refill evacuated regions.

This changes both parts of the escaping ionising emissivity. Feedback regulates the young stars that produce Lyman-continuum photons and restructures the neutral gas that determines how many of those photons escape. The same process redistributes metals and angular momentum, linking reionisation to galaxy morphology, dust attenuation, and line emission.

Because the remaining physics and numerics are held fixed, differences between matched haloes can be read as the response to the adopted supernova timing and energy distribution, within the assumptions of the common galaxy-formation model.

Held fixed

Initial conditions and galaxy model

Corresponding haloes form from the same phases and amplitudes in the initial density field. Cosmology, volume, refinement, hydrodynamics, thermochemistry, subgrid turbulence, star formation, stellar spectra, and radiation transport are shared.

Changed

Supernova timing and energy

Bursty-SN synchronises explosions at 10 Myr, Smooth-SN samples delay times from 3 to 40 Myr, and Hyper-SN adds a metallicity-dependent 1052 erg channel to the distributed timing model.

Compared

Galaxies and ionised gas

The analysis follows star-formation duty cycles, gas and stellar kinematics, metal transport, outflows, ionising-photon production and escape, synthetic observables, and the growth and overlap of ionised regions.

02 · Numerical framework

From cosmological initial conditions to adaptive meshes.

A cosmological radiation-hydrodynamics calculation

RAMSES-RT evolves dark matter, gas, stars, and radiation together on an adaptive mesh.

The initial conditions are generated at z = 30 with MUSIC2-monofonIC, using second-order perturbation theory and a fixed-amplitude method. The periodic volume is 10 cMpc/h on a side and contains 512³ dark-matter particles.

Hydrodynamics

Gas dynamics

A second-order MUSCL–Hancock Godunov scheme uses an HLLC Riemann solver, a MinMod slope limiter, and an ideal-gas adiabatic index of 5/3.

Gravity

Dark matter, gas, and stars

RAMSES solves self-gravity for the gas and collisionless particles. Haloes and galaxies are identified with AdaptaHOP.

Refinement

Adaptive mesh

Cells refine when their mass exceeds the quasi-Lagrangian criterion or when the local Jeans length would span fewer than eight cells.

03 · Galaxy formation model

Cooling, star formation, radiation, and feedback.

Baryonic physics and the star-formation cycle

The galaxy model combines non-equilibrium thermochemistry, adaptive hydrodynamics, unresolved turbulent energy, star-particle formation, stellar spectra, radiation transport, and mechanical supernova feedback.

Dark matter builds the gravitational potential. Gas enters haloes through filaments and mergers, is shock heated, and cools into the interstellar medium. Hydrogen and helium ionisation states are evolved out of equilibrium and coupled to the local radiation field. Metal-line cooling is tabulated above 104 K; fine-structure cooling allows enriched gas to cool to about 300 K.

The mesh resolves structures down to tens of parsecs, but not individual star-forming clouds or all stages of a supernova remnant. Unresolved density fluctuations, turbulent support, star formation, and remnant evolution are represented by local subgrid prescriptions. Galaxy morphology and star-formation history are not imposed.

Model sequenceFrom gas supply to photon escapeFollow the six-stage baryon cycle used in SPICE.
  1. 01

    Halo growth and gas supply

    Dark-matter haloes grow through mergers and smooth accretion. Filaments supply gas and angular momentum.

  2. 02

    Cooling and phase structure

    Non-equilibrium H/He chemistry, photoheating, collisional processes, Compton exchange, and metal cooling determine the gas temperature and ionisation state.

  3. 03

    Mesh refinement

    Mass and Jeans-length criteria refine collapsing regions. The Jeans length is kept across at least eight cells until the maximum refinement level is reached.

  4. 04

    Star formation

    Gas with nH ≥ 10 cm−3 follows a multi-freefall Schmidt law. The local efficiency depends on turbulent Mach number and virial parameter.

  5. 05

    Stellar particles

    Star particles are sampled in multiples of 970 M⊙. Each represents a coeval population with a Chabrier IMF and BPASS v2.2.1 spectra.

  6. 06

    Radiation and supernovae

    Stellar particles emit photons and later return momentum, energy, mass, and metals. The response of the gas regulates subsequent star formation and photon escape.

01

Local Schmidt law

Eligible gas forms stars with a variable efficiency per free-fall time.

Here ρ is the cell gas density. The efficiency εff is calculated from the collapsing fraction of the unresolved density distribution.

Multi-freefall efficiency

A lognormal subgrid density distribution sets εff from turbulent compression and gravitational boundedness.

SPICE adopts ε = 0.5 and φ = 1/0.49. The formulation admits collapse in globally unstable subsonic cells (αvir < 1) and in overdense fluctuations generated by supersonic turbulence.

04 · Stellar feedback

The controlled variable in the flagship suite.

Three ways to deliver supernova energy

All three runs use the same mechanical coupling scheme. The explosion times and event energies differ.

The mechanical scheme distributes ejecta mass, metals, thermal energy, and radial momentum over the host cell and its 48 neighbours. For each coupling direction, the swept-to-ejecta mass ratio χ is compared with a density-, energy-, and metallicity-dependent transition χtr. If the Sedov–Taylor phase is resolved, the energy-conserving momentum is injected; otherwise the algorithm supplies the asymptotic snowplough momentum, avoiding numerical overcooling of an unresolved remnant.

A Chabrier IMF gives 0.01639 core-collapse supernovae per solar mass, or 15 events for a minimum-mass stellar particle. The recycled mass fraction is ηSN = 0.31, of which 5% is assigned to newly synthesised metals.

Bursty-SNOne coherent event at 10 Myr

Every supernova from a stellar particle occurs at once, with 2 × 1051 erg assigned to each event.

Smooth-SNEvents distributed from 3 to 40 Myr

The same energy is delivered event by event, with delay times drawn from the STARBURST99 supernova rate.

Hyper-SNDistributed timing, variable energy

Ordinary events span 1050–2 × 1051 erg, while a metallicity-dependent fraction reach 1052 erg.

05 · Radiation

How starlight travels through gas and dust.

Five photon groups, evolved on the fly

RAMSES-RT transports radiation alongside the gas, allowing ionising photons and radiation pressure to respond to the evolving galaxies.

Radiation is solved with a first-order Godunov method, M1 moment closure, and a Global Lax–Friedrichs flux. Stellar luminosities and spectra depend on particle age, mass, and metallicity and are taken from BPASS v2.2.1 for a Chabrier initial mass function.

The model includes direct radiation pressure and multi-scattering infrared pressure on dust. Dust abundance scales with gas metallicity and neutral fraction. Transport adopts a reduced speed of light of 0.1c for computational tractability, while radiation-pressure forces use the full speed of light; up to 500 radiation subcycles may be taken within one hydrodynamic step.

07 · Analysis and scope

What is catalogued, and how results should be read.

Resolved structures and known limits

The volume resolves the internal structure of early galaxies and their local ionised environments, but not every scale relevant to reionisation.

AdaptaHOP identifies haloes containing at least 100 dark-matter particles and galaxies containing at least 100 stellar particles. These catalogues support comparisons of galaxy abundance, star-formation histories, morphology, ionising-photon escape, and observable emission across the three runs.

Finite volume

A 10 cMpc/h box resolves galaxy-scale physics well, but is too small to deliver a converged cosmic-mean reionisation history or capture the largest late-time ionised regions.

Model dependence

Star formation, unresolved turbulence, mechanical feedback, stellar spectra, and dust all rely on numerical or subgrid assumptions. The comparison measures the consequences of those choices; it does not remove them.

Radiation approximation

The reduced speed of light is accurate while ionisation fronts remain slower than 0.1c, but may become limiting as fronts accelerate through voids near the end of reionisation.

Missing physics

The flagship calculations do not include active-galactic-nucleus feedback, cosmic rays, or magnetic fields, each of which may alter the interstellar medium and galaxy observables.
