Simulating the early Universe

The SPICE simulation suite

Impact of stellar feedback on the first galaxies and cosmic reionisation.

JWST is observing galaxies less than a billion years after the Big Bang. SPICE simulates this period, following the growth of early galaxies and the ultraviolet radiation that ionised the hydrogen between them.

The flagship calculations evolve dark matter, gas, stars, chemistry, and radiation in a 10 cMpc/h periodic volume from z = 30 to z = 5. The finest cells reach approximately 28 physical parsecs at z = 5.

The three runs start from the same cosmological initial conditions and use the same galaxy-formation model. They differ in the timing and energy of supernova explosions. With the other inputs fixed, differences in star formation, galaxy structure, metal transport, ionising-photon escape, and reionisation can be compared directly. Synthetic observations are compared with measurements from JWST, ALMA, the VLT, and other facilities.

Why SPICE?

A virtual laboratory for galaxies and reionisation

Cosmic reionisation was not driven by photon production alone. Ionising radiation from young stars first had to cross a dense, chemically evolving interstellar medium, enter the circumgalactic gas, and only then contribute to the ionisation of intergalactic hydrogen. The relevant source term therefore depends on both the stellar ionising luminosity and the fraction of photons that escape from each galaxy.

Stellar feedback couples these quantities. Photoheating, radiation pressure, winds, and supernovae regulate the conversion of gas into stars, while also changing the density distribution and covering fraction of neutral gas around those stars. The same feedback event can reduce the instantaneous star-formation rate but increase the escaping ionising emissivity by opening low-column-density channels.

Observations measure the light that survives this process, not the complete gas history that produced it. Accretion, turbulent collapse, enrichment, dust attenuation, resonant scattering, and instrumental selection all enter the comparison. SPICE follows these processes in a common radiation-hydrodynamic framework and changes the supernova prescription while keeping the initial conditions and the rest of the galaxy model fixed.

The discriminating question

With the broader galaxy-formation model fixed, which properties of early galaxies retain a measurable memory of stellar feedback? Which familiar observables remain degenerate across different supernova timing and energy prescriptions?

Controlled simulation experiment

Matched initial conditions, three feedback models

All three flagship runs use the same volume, initial density field, refinement strategy, radiation transport, chemistry, and star-formation model. They therefore form corresponding haloes in the same cosmic environments.

The controlled variable is how core-collapse supernova energy is distributed in time and, for Hyper-SN, across event energies. Comparing matched galaxies reveals how the temporal coherence of feedback changes gas retention, metal transport, disc formation, ionising escape, and the topology of reionisation.

M01

Bursty-SN

All core-collapse supernovae associated with a stellar particle explode together at an age of 10 Myr, with 2 × 1051 erg assigned to each event. The temporal clustering produces rapid pressurisation, strong outflows, and repeated disruption of dense star-forming gas.

Coherent energy injection
M02

Smooth-SN

Explosion times are sampled between 3 and 40 Myr from the STARBURST99 supernova rate, again using 2 × 1051 erg per event. Energy is delivered less coherently, allowing gas to remain concentrated and massive rotationally supported systems to assemble more readily.

Distributed energy injection
M03

Hyper-SN

Explosion times are distributed as in Smooth-SN, but event energies vary and a metallicity-dependent fraction reach 1052 erg. This tests whether rare hypernovae in metal-poor stellar populations can modify early enrichment and outflows without imposing synchronous explosions.

Research projects

Tracing feedback from galaxies to reionisation

The primary studies follow the feedback signal from the dynamics of simulated galaxies to quantities measured by JWST, ALMA, and ground-based spectroscopy. They test galaxy morphology, ionising escape, Lyα radiative transfer, ultraviolet variability, and metal-line emission.

Each project page explains the numerical or observational construction of the diagnostic, guides the reader through a central figure, and discusses results and limitations from the full paper. The aim is to distinguish a robust physical inference from a trend that depends on aperture, line selection, sample definition, or unresolved modelling assumptions.

Feedback · Morphology · Reionisation
Bhagwat et al. · 2024

Feedback and cosmic reionisation

Changing the temporal clustering of supernovae reorganises the interstellar and circumgalactic gas. Bursty feedback produces more dispersion-supported systems and substantially larger Lyman-continuum escape fractions, allowing reionisation to proceed earlier even without the largest intrinsic photon budget.

Lyα · Offsets · Transfer
Bhagwat et al. · 2025

Interpreting Lyα at z > 5

Resonant transfer through the simulated multiphase gas commonly displaces the emergent Lyα peak from the compact ultraviolet source. Mock JWST and ground-based observations then show how that physical offset, extended emission, and slit placement alter the recovered line flux and equivalent width.

UVLF · Variability · JWST
Basu et al. · 2026

UV luminosity-function variability

Following the same galaxy population over 10, 30, and 50 Myr separates cosmological growth from short-lived luminous phases. Variability is strongest in shallow potential wells and in Bursty-SN, broadening the range of halo masses represented in a UV-selected sample.

[O III] · JWST · ALMA
Casavecchia et al. · 2026

[O III] as a feedback diagnostic

Cell-by-cell ionisation and statistical-equilibrium calculations predict the optical 5008 Å and far-infrared 88 μm transitions. Their luminosity functions and resolved morphologies retain feedback-dependent differences that are less apparent in the dust-attenuated ultraviolet continuum.

[C II] · Star formation · Kinematics
Bhagwat et al. · 2026

[C II] in the first galaxies

Equilibrium carbon ionisation and collisional excitation are used to construct ALMA-like 158 μm datacubes. [C II] maps the extended cool neutral reservoir and the emergence of rotational support, but remains faint in much of the hot, rapidly outflowing gas and can severely underestimate wind mass loading.

Science highlights

From feedback to observables

SPICE directly evolves density, temperature, velocity, ionisation state, stellar age, and metallicity. Telescopes instead record photons after transfer through gas and dust, projection onto the sky, finite resolution, aperture losses, noise, and sample selection.

Those steps can change the comparison. Lyα is scattered away from its production site, line emission weights particular gas phases, and a slit or surface-brightness threshold may miss extended light. An intrinsic luminosity or star-formation rate is not automatically equivalent to the value reported in an observing catalogue.

We assign spectra to star particles using their ages and metallicities, calculate line emissivities from the gas state, and propagate photons through the simulated galaxies. The resulting images, spectra, and datacubes are then convolved and sampled with the relevant filters, apertures, beams, and spectral channels.

01

Evolve the source

Resolve the physical state

SPICE follows gas density, temperature, velocity, metallicity, ionisation, radiation, and coeval stellar populations. These fields can be compared at fixed initial conditions across the three feedback runs.

02

Generate and transport light

Generate observable photons

BPASS populations supply age- and metallicity-dependent continua; atomic calculations assign metal-line emissivities cell by cell; RASCAS propagates UV and Lyα photons through gas and dust along multiple sightlines.

03

Measure the mock data

Recover telescope measurements

Synthetic images, spectra, and datacubes are passed through filters, slits, apertures, ALMA-like beams, velocity channels, and noise before luminosities, equivalent widths, sizes, and kinematics are recovered.

Forward-modelled diagnostics

One galaxy, a multi-wavelength view

Each tracer weights a different part of the galaxy. Comparing several of them shows which differences come from the feedback model and which come from the measurement.

01UV continuum

Construct Rest-frame 1500 Å light is assembled from BPASS v2.2.1 stellar populations and attenuated through the simulated dust and metal distribution along many rays.

Measure Dust-attenuated magnitudes, luminosity functions, morphologies, and short-timescale variability connect feedback to JWST-selected galaxy populations.

02Lyα

Construct Photon packets from recombination, collisional excitation, and the stellar continuum scatter through neutral hydrogen, deuterium, and dust with RASCAS.

Measure Multi-angle datacubes are sampled with JWST micro-shutter and ground-based slit geometries, revealing offsets, extended haloes, flux losses, and equivalent-width bias.

03[O III]

Construct Oxygen ionisation balance and five-level O++ statistical equilibrium provide cell-by-cell emissivities for 5008 Å, 4960 Å, and 88 μm emission.

Measure Optical and far-infrared luminosity functions, maps, sizes, and luminosity-weighted kinematics link the same ion to complementary JWST and ALMA regimes.

04[C II]

Construct The C I–C II–C III balance and collisional excitation by electrons and neutral hydrogen determine the 158 μm emissivity of every gas cell.

Measure ALMA-like cubes include a 0.3-arcsec beam, 30 km s−1 channels, and noise before moment maps, radial profiles, and line components are inferred.
